## Overview

*Designed with Aster Dean at **MITES** (MIT Introduction to Technology,
Engineering, and Science), summer 2024.*

**The Grove** is a public pavilion sited on the bank of the Charles River. The
brief was open — design something for a real site — so we picked the stretch of
riverbank between the MIT campus and Storrow Drive, and designed for the people
already using it: cyclists, runners, sailors, and anyone walking the bike path.

The design promotes *reconnection between the body and mind*. It takes its
shape and structure from the trees already on the site: the walls are **spaced-out
vertical wooden slats**, so the pavilion reads as an extension of the tree line
rather than an object dropped onto it. You see through it from every angle.

![The finished model seen from the river side, its slatted walls rising off wooden stepping stones set into the water.](/images/projects/architecture/arch-13.jpg)

## The design

The pavilion is two storeys, and each one does a different job:

- **Ground floor — communal and open.** Anyone passing can stay and hang out or
  simply walk straight through. A **light fixture of recycled stained glass**
  hangs at the centre and doubles as a rainwater collector. A **root-like deck**
  spreads out into the water so people can sit and dangle their feet in.
- **Upper floor — quiet and elevated.** Reached by a rope ladder *or* a
  wheelchair-accessible ramp, it's dedicated to viewing the water from height.
  The space can be rented for dances, concerts, and dates.

Materials were chosen to be salvaged wherever possible: **reclaimed wood,
reclaimed stained glass, rocks, steel attachment brackets, plexiglass, and steel
piping.**

![Detail of the recycled stained-glass light fixture, a cluster of pink, orange, and yellow panels suspended beneath the roof opening.](/images/projects/architecture/arch-04.jpg)

## Site analysis

Before designing anything we documented the site: sun path, prevailing wind,
noise sources, and the notable locations around it — the dock, the bike path,
Storrow Drive, and the Boston skyline across the water.

Two findings shaped the design. The site sits under a **constant flow of
cyclists, walkers, and scooters**, so the ground floor had to be genuinely
walk-through rather than a destination you commit to entering. And Boston
summers are hot — regularly into the 90s °F — which made **shade and access to
the water** the priorities rather than enclosure.

We also read the site's history: the Charles is named for King Charles I of
England, and the riverbank was industrial and commercial through the 19th
century before **Charles Eliot's redesign in 1894** turned it into a public
centrepiece for the city. Designing a public room on that bank felt like a
continuation of that move.

![The two presentation boards — “The Grove” with the concept, materials, and site analysis on the left, and the architectural drawings on the right.](/images/projects/architecture/arch-12.jpg)

## Drawings

The set covers two elevations — the **water view** and the **bike path view** —
a section cut through both floors, and plans for the upper and bottom floors.
The plans are where the slat geometry gets resolved: the outline is an
irregular, cloud-like curve, and every slat is a separate cut following it.

## Building the model

The final model is laser-cut plywood on a foam terrain base, with a **poured
acrylic river** and free-standing wooden stepping stones set into it.

The slats were the hard part. Each one is individually cut and glued upright
along a curved edge, and they have to stay parallel while the glue sets — so
most of the build was jigs and painter's tape holding pieces square. The curved
elevated ramp is bent plexiglass on dowel piers.

## The review

We presented in **MIT's Lobby 10**, boards up behind the model, to visitors and
the MITES faculty. The best part was watching people crouch to model eye level
and look *through* the slats — which is exactly the thing the drawings can't
show you.

![Ava presenting The Grove to a group in MIT's Lobby 10, gesturing at the site analysis board with the model on the table.](/images/projects/architecture/arch-01.jpg)

## What I'd change

The slat spacing is uniform all the way round. If I did it again I'd **vary the
density** — tighter where the pavilion faces Storrow Drive to cut road noise,
looser on the water side where the view is the whole point. The model makes that
obvious in a way the plan drawings didn't.
